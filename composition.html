Composition & lighting

Turn Ordinary Scenes into Stronger Photographs

Composition controls where the viewer looks, while lighting shapes mood, texture, and detail.

These are creative tools, not strict rules. Use them to make deliberate choices, then break them when the story needs something different.

Try a composition technique

Composition techniques

Guide the viewer through the frame

Rule of thirds

Place key details near a third-line or intersection to create balance.

Tip: Turn on your camera grid and place eyes near the upper third.

Leading lines

Roads, railings, and shadows can lead the eye to a subject.

Tip: Start a line near a lower edge for a stronger entry point.

Framing

Use windows, arches, branches, or doorways to surround the subject.

Tip: Keep the frame darker or softer than the subject where possible.

Symmetry

Mirrored shapes and centred structure can feel calm, formal, or powerful.

Tip: Stand square to the scene and check both edges before shooting.

Negative space

Empty space gives a subject room to breathe and strengthens its importance.

Tip: Move closer or use a clean wall or sky as your background.

Depth and layering

Foreground, middle ground, and background make a flat image feel spacious.

Tip: Include a nearby object to give scale to distant scenery.

Patterns and repetition

Repeated shapes create rhythm; one different element can become the subject.

Tip: Fill the frame, then look for the break in the pattern.

Perspective and angle

Camera height changes the relationship between objects and the feeling of a scene.

Tip: Try low, eye-level, and high viewpoints before choosing one.

Lighting techniques

Read the light before you press the shutter

Natural light

Window light and outdoor light can be soft, directional, and easy to observe.

Golden hour

Low sun near sunrise or sunset creates warm colour, long shadows, and softer contrast.

Blue hour

The period around dawn or dusk gives cool ambient light and can balance city lights.

Harsh midday light

Overhead sun creates strong shadows; use shade, graphic contrast, or a reflector to manage it.

Soft light

Large or diffused sources create gentle transitions and are often flattering for portraits.

Hard light

Small or direct sources create crisp shadows, bold texture, and a more dramatic mood.

Light direction

Front, side, and backlighting

Front lighting

Light from behind the camera reduces shadows and shows clear detail, but can look flatter with less texture.

Side lighting

Light from the side creates shadows and texture, giving a subject depth and a more dramatic mood.

Backlighting

Light behind a subject can create rim light and separation; expose carefully to avoid a silhouette unless that is intentional.

Common mistakes

Small checks, better photographs

Most issues are easier to fix before you take the picture. Pause, look around the edges, and take one deliberate second shot.

Busy objects can compete with a subject. Move your feet, simplify the angle, or use a wider aperture to soften the background.

A tilted horizon can feel accidental. Use the grid, level the camera, and straighten subtly in editing if needed.

A subject can look trapped by an edge or cut off at a joint. Reframe with intentional space and check the whole border.

Bright areas can lose detail. Lower exposure, protect the highlights, or change position to avoid direct glare.

When subject and background are similar, they merge. Change angle, add side light, use a contrasting background, or adjust depth of field.

Empty space should support the subject, not happen by accident. Crop tighter or leave space in the direction a person is looking or moving.

Light changes faces, shadows, and colour. Walk around the subject and choose the direction that supports the mood you want.

Centred framing can be powerful, but doing it every time can make images predictable. Test an off-centre composition too.

Try it yourself

Photograph one subject four ways

Choose one subject and make four deliberate photographs: one using rule of thirds, one using symmetry, one using leading lines, and one with a different lighting direction. Compare where your eye goes in each image.
